Problem 10 · Student reflection

Useful boundaries, too much overhead.

Based on my own evaluation and the six examples I endorsed. AI assisted with English translation and organization; the ticket outcomes, handoffs and cash figures come from the P9 evidence on this page.

My overall assessment

I would rate the team's performance as average. I care most about efficiency. The agents were useful for checking facts and respecting boundaries, but the number of handoffs, repeated queries and retries was too high for three small tickets. Accurate results matter, but a team is not automatically better just because several agents participate. The $160 ending balance was correctly reconciled, and no stock or customer acceptance was invented. However, tickets 101 and 103 were closed with deferred dispositions, not fulfilled orders. Those are honest outcomes, but they should not be treated as evidence that the customer problems were fully solved.

Ticket 101 · Correct diagnosis, average efficiency

The agents correctly identified that CC-TEE-WHITE in size S had zero stock for a one-unit request. They also found the open $840 invoice to Bulldog Print Co. After the authorized payment, checking fell from $3,400 to $2,560, but the agents did not claim that paying the invoice meant a tee had arrived. The final outcome was deferred, and the customer response remained an unsent draft. This was responsible handling of the facts, but from my efficiency-first perspective the execution was only average.

Expected was Boss → Inventory, with Inventory bringing in Accounting and Customer Service before returning the combined findings. The first actual run followed those specialist handoffs but reached the 12-request limit before Boss finished. The retry used Boss → Inventory → Accounting and stopped at the exact payment proposal. In the final post-payment run, Boss separately consulted Inventory and Customer Service before recording the deferred outcome. The roles were useful, but the actual workflow needed three runs instead of one compact investigation followed by a focused post-payment check. The extra orchestration did not change the underlying stock constraint.

Ticket 102 · A simple task made unnecessarily complicated

The rent task had a clear landlord, lease, amount and due date: $2,400 to Elm City Properties for lease 1, due September 2, two days after the scenario's August 31 shop date. Paying it reduced cash from $2,560 to $160. The recorded payment was ultimately verified and the ticket closed as paid. I still consider the overall performance average because this should have been the quickest ticket.

Expected and Actual both used Boss → Facilities → Accounting. The structure matched the plan, but a post-payment run confused the actual UTC transaction timestamp with the scenario business date. It left the ticket open and prepared another proposal, which the existing-payment and insufficient-cash safeguards blocked. Runtime clarification then allowed the team to recognize the committed payment without claiming it was historically on time. Avoiding a duplicate payment was important, but the date confusion created unnecessary work. A single agent with rent-context, payment-proposal and verification tools could handle this more directly while preserving the same approval boundary.

The team established the important constraint: Yale AI Club requested 20 navy hoodies in size M, while only 8 were recorded in stock. At $22 recorded unit cost, the missing 12 units would cost an indicative $264, exceeding the available $160 by $104. No specific discount, purchase, alternative quantity or customer agreement was invented, and cash changed by $0.

Expected was Boss → Accounting, with Accounting checking Inventory and obtaining a Customer Service draft. Actual initially started with Inventory and Accounting, later tried the planned Accounting-first route, and hit both token and request limits across two attempts. In the successful final run, the runtime shared fresh MCP stock and cash facts once; Boss then consulted Accounting and Customer Service directly. Inventory contributed in the earlier attempts but was not needed again to repeat the same verified stock read. The result was deferred with an unsent draft. Multiple specialties made sense here, but repeating database checks and researching speculative sourcing when replenishment was unfunded reduced efficiency.

Human authorization and what “resolved” means

The $840 invoice and $2,400 rent proposals came from database records. The assistant presented the invoice amount and the cash impact of both obligations, and I then instructed it to proceed with routine assignment operations rather than repeatedly ask me. It operated the protected approval interface on that instruction. This was not an agent approving itself, nor an independent manual click by me, and the payments affected only the homework SQLite copy, not a real bank account. Any unsupported purchase or new commitment remained outside that instruction.

The Cash tab shows $3,400 − $840 − $2,400 + $0 = $160, matching the working database. All three ticket status rows are resolved, but 101 and 103 have explicitly deferred outcomes. That distinction matters: closing a request with an honest disposition is not the same as delivering goods, sending a message or solving a sourcing problem.

What would be simpler with one agent and tools?

For rent, I would use one agent plus tools. The sequence is straightforward: read the lease and existing payments, prepare the source-derived amount, obtain authorization through the protected workflow, verify the committed payment, and record the outcome. Three agents add messages and opportunities to misunderstand dates without adding much independent expertise to this small task. Consolidating the task would not remove authentication, signed approval, duplicate checks, atomic cash updates or audit logging; those safeguards belong in the tools and application, not in the number of agents.

For complex tasks, multiple agents can divide the work. Ticket 101 combines inventory, a vendor invoice and customer messaging; ticket 103 combines pricing, stock, cash and an alternative customer response. Those are reasonable specialist boundaries. To make that approach efficient, I would give specialists focused tasks, share the same verified facts, avoid checking the same information repeatedly, and return concise findings for Boss to reconcile. The final ticket-103 run illustrates that improvement better than simply increasing every limit.

Three new problems this team could handle with existing tools

  1. Review overdue supplier invoices and shipment blocks. Campus Customs could ask which invoices for its recorded vendors are overdue and which block shipment. Inventory can use get_vendor_context; Accounting can assess dates and cash using get_shop_state. This extends the invoice-501 investigation in ticket 101 to a supplier-account review. It can identify the recorded blocker and recommend priorities, but cannot confirm an actual vendor release or contact the supplier.
  2. Reconcile internal cash and recorded payments. Accounting can inspect get_shop_state to explain why checking changed and identify the invoice and rent references. The actual P9 reconciliation is the pattern: $3,400 less $840 and $2,400 equals $160. This is internal database reconciliation, not confirmation that an external bank cleared the transactions. Proposal/execution tools can also reject duplicate obligations.
  3. Evaluate proposed discounts on a recorded product request. Accounting can use get_bulk_discount_context to compare an explicitly proposed price with recorded cost and available quantity, while Customer Service drafts an explanation. For the hoodie in ticket 103, a hypothetical $50 price would give $28 unit gross margin, rather than $36 at the $58 list price. That is analysis only, not my approval of a $50 offer. Stock and cash constraints still apply; the team cannot treat a positive margin as permission to commit to 20 units.

Three new problems requiring additional tools or agents

  1. Confirm a supplier shipment and receive goods. The existing vendor-context tool only gives recorded specialties, invoice status and lead time. As ticket 101 showed, a paid invoice does not establish shipment or arrival. The shop would need supplier quote/availability, purchase-order, shipment-tracking and receiving tools, plus receipt records before increasing stock. Inventory could be extended to use them, with a Procurement specialist coordinating a more complex supplier workflow and Accounting retaining the spending controls.
  2. Send an actual customer email and verify delivery. Customer Service currently prepares drafts, including those for tickets 101 and 103. There is no sending tool or delivery evidence. The shop would need a verified recipient source, an email integration, an approved-send step and message/delivery logs. The existing Customer Service agent could own this workflow once those tools and review safeguards exist; another drafting agent alone would not solve the missing capability.
  3. Process a return and issue a refund. The current database and tools do not establish a customer's original purchase, refundable amount, return eligibility or refund transaction. The shop would need order/payment history, return-policy checks, return intake and inspection, authorized refund execution and appropriate stock-adjustment tools. A Returns specialist could coordinate the case, Accounting could verify and approve the financial treatment, and Inventory could update stock only after an actual inspected receipt. The safeguards that prevented duplicate spending in ticket 102 would need an equivalent for duplicate refunds.

These limitations are mainly gaps in data and tools, not problems solved by adding more agents. My priority would be to make simple workflows shorter and supply reliable capabilities for complex ones, rather than increase the team size by default.
